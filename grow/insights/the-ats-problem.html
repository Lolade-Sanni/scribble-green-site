The ATS Problem: Why 75% of CVs Never Reach a Human Eye

Steven Umurie
CTO, Scribble Green
| April 2026 · 10 min read

Most job seekers assume their CV is being evaluated by a recruiter. In reality, it is usually evaluated by software first. Applicant Tracking Systems (ATS) act as the first filter — and most CVs do not pass.

This isn't because candidates are unqualified. It's because their CVs are not structured in a way that machines can interpret effectively. Research shows that up to 75% of applications are rejected by ATS before a human ever sees them.

What an ATS Actually Does

An ATS scans CVs for keywords, structure, and relevance. It ranks candidates based on how well their documents match the job description. The software parses your CV into a structured database, extracting key information like:

  • Contact information
  • Work experience and dates
  • Education and qualifications
  • Skills and certifications
  • Keyword matches against the job description

This means your CV is not being "read" in the traditional sense. It is being parsed, scored, and filtered before a human ever sees it. The system assigns a score based on how closely your CV aligns with the role requirements, and only candidates above a certain threshold move forward.

"If your CV is not written for both humans and machines, it will likely reach neither."

Why Most CVs Fail

Understanding why CVs fail is the first step to fixing the problem. Here are the most common reasons candidates are filtered out:

  • Poor formatting that ATS cannot parse — complex layouts, text boxes, and images confuse the software
  • Missing or weak keyword alignment — not using the language and terminology from the job description
  • Use of graphics, tables, or columns — these break parsing algorithms and cause important information to be lost
  • Generic content — a one-size-fits-all approach that lacks role-specific relevance and doesn't address the employer's needs
  • Incorrect file format — some ATS systems struggle with certain file types; .docx and .pdf are generally safest
  • Missing standard sections — not using conventional headings like "Experience" or "Education" makes parsing difficult

Even highly qualified candidates get filtered out at this stage simply because their CVs are not machine-readable. A study by TopResume found that 98% of Fortune 500 companies use ATS, making it essential to understand how these systems work.

How ATS Scoring Works

ATS systems typically score CVs based on several factors. Understanding these can help you optimise effectively:

  • Keyword density — how frequently relevant terms appear in your CV (but beware of keyword stuffing)
  • Keyword proximity — how closely related keywords appear together in context
  • Section relevance — whether keywords appear in appropriate sections (e.g., "Python" in skills vs. hobbies)
  • Work history patterns — length of employment, career progression, and gaps
  • Education relevance — matching qualifications and institutions to requirements

The system also considers the overall structure and layout. A clean, well-organised CV with standard headings will generally score higher than a visually complex one, even if the content is the same.

Keywords Are Not Optional

ATS systems rely heavily on keywords. These are typically pulled directly from the job description — skills, tools, qualifications, and responsibilities. But there's a right way and a wrong way to use them.

The right way: Use keywords naturally within meaningful descriptions of your experience. For example, instead of just listing "Python" as a skill, you might write: "Developed data analysis pipelines using Python, Pandas, and NumPy to automate reporting processes."

The wrong way: Keyword stuffing — cramming as many keywords as possible without meaningful context. This is not only ineffective but may also get your CV flagged as spam by more sophisticated systems.

Pro tip: Copy the job description text into a word cloud generator to identify the most frequently used terms. These are your priority keywords.

Structure Matters More Than Design

Many candidates prioritise visual appeal over functionality. While design matters for human readers, ATS systems prefer simplicity. Here's what works best:

  • Use standard section headings — Experience, Education, Skills, Certifications, etc.
  • Avoid complex layouts — no tables, columns, text boxes, or graphics
  • Stick to clean, linear formatting — left-to-right, top-to-bottom reading order
  • Use standard fonts — Arial, Calibri, or Times New Roman are safest
  • Keep file names professional — "Steven_Umurie_CV.docx" is better than "CV2_FINAL.docx"

In testing, a well-structured CV will outperform a visually impressive but unreadable one. The ideal CV is one that works for both machines and humans.

The Human Layer Still Exists

Passing the ATS is only the first step. Once your CV reaches a recruiter, it still needs to communicate value clearly and quickly. This is where many candidates make a second mistake — they optimise for ATS but forget the human reader.

For the human layer, focus on:

  • Clear, compelling storytelling in your professional summary
  • Quantifiable achievements (e.g., "Increased sales by 30% in 6 months")
  • Relevant experience presented in reverse chronological order
  • A clean, readable layout with adequate white space
  • Error-free writing — typos and grammar mistakes undermine credibility

Common ATS Myths Debunked

There's a lot of misinformation about ATS. Let's clear up some common myths:

  • Myth: "ATS can't read PDFs." Reality: Modern ATS can read PDFs, but they must be text-based, not scanned images.
  • Myth: "You need to include every keyword from the job description." Reality: Use keywords that genuinely apply to your experience. Forced inclusion is obvious and counterproductive.
  • Myth: "Longer CVs are better." Reality: Most ATS systems prefer conciseness. Two pages is typically the ideal length.
  • Myth: "Creative design helps you stand out." Reality: For ATS-filtered roles, simple and standard is more effective.

What a Strong CV Does

The most effective CVs operate on two levels:

  • Level 1: ATS optimisation — it is structured, keyword-rich, and machine-readable
  • Level 2: Human appeal — it is compelling, achievement-focused, and visually clean

This balance is what separates candidates who get shortlisted from those who don't. A well-optimised CV increases your chances of moving through the ATS filter and making a strong impression on a human reader.

Practical Steps to Optimise Your CV

  1. Analyse the job description — identify key skills, qualifications, and responsibilities
  2. Match your experience — use similar language and terminology where applicable
  3. Standardise your format — use clean, simple layouts with standard headings
  4. Use both long and short versions of keywords — e.g., "Project Management" and "Agile Project Management"
  5. Include a skills section — clearly list relevant technical and soft skills
  6. Quantify achievements — use numbers, percentages, and metrics where possible
  7. Test your CV — use free tools like Jobscan or ResyMatch to check ATS compatibility
  8. Get feedback — have someone review your CV for clarity and impact

Final Thought

The hiring process has changed, but most CVs haven't. Understanding how ATS systems work is no longer optional — it is a basic requirement for navigating modern recruitment. The job market is competitive enough; don't let your CV be filtered out because of technical incompatibility.

Invest the time to optimise your CV for both machines and humans. It's one of the most high-impact actions you can take to improve your job search outcomes.
